How It Started

Our Story

01
The Problem

Good Gear Helps Good Wrestling.

Cotton robes absorb sweat and hold it. By your second match they're heavy, cold against your skin, and fighting your ability to stay warm between bouts. Throughout his career, Mo tried to stay warm between matches to keep his muscles agile and ready to go. But no product really carved out a niche as a solution to this problem. That's what this started as: a straightforward question about solving that problem and whether better materials could make a real difference.

On the mat — where the problem was first noticed
02
Old Problem, New Solution

Old Problem, New Solution

I met Mo a few months prior after signing up for some private wrestling lessons with him. When he told me about the idea for a garment specifically for wrestlers, it struck a chord. I had spent quite a bit of time working in manufacturing and took a huge interest in international trade. I'd even written a book on international trade and trade policy as it related almost solely to manufacturing. This was a chance for me to put rubber to the road and get real experience firsthand having something manufactured myself.

I didn't want to make a cheap product, and I didn't want to just use fabric that was said to manage moisture well. I wanted something I knew would work well, and better than what people typically use. With wrestlers going on the mat, then coming back sweaty to put it on, then back to the mat and back again to the garment, I knew I also needed something special. It couldn't be cotton like a bath towel because you have to put it back on, not just soak up moisture once. I knew it couldn't be typical exercise fabric that's thin because it needs to soak up sweat. It needs to soak moisture like a bath towel, but dry quickly like a thin garment.

Wrestlers need gear that works between matches, not just for one
03
The Research

All-Nighters and Academic Papers

Universities and other groups perform research in textile science, and I found moisture management to be a specific area of study. But there were thousands upon thousands of papers — too many for me to go through manually. So I used my background in computing to build a program that searched for papers focused on moisture management, extract performance metrics like drying time and others, and put it all in spreadsheet format where I could simply sort the data from largest to smallest or vice versa depending on if low or high scores were the better scores. This is where one thing kept popping up: electrospun nanofiber fabric — something made in labs, not in the typical knitting and weaving mills you think of for textiles.

The image to the left is what that material looks like under a scanning electron microscope. You can immediately see something different is going on by how there is no clear "locking" of the fibers like you'd see in a woven or knit fabric. It's a continuous web of sub-micron fibers — that is, fibers hundreds of times thinner than a human hair. The tiny fiber creates capillary force that drives moisture out at a level you can't get with traditional fabric.

Fundamentally different from knits and wovens. This is what's in your robe.

04
The Build

Revision After Revision Until It Was Right

Research is one thing, but handing someone a garment to use before and between wrestling matches is another. Mo and I spent a significant amount of time on the first prototype. We had that made right here in Colorado Springs by a garment maker that'd worked at Northface and had a long history in textiles. We wanted it to look just like we'd envisioned it. And we wanted no PFAS and no fluorinated chemistry. Once we had the prototype, we had something that had the look, but not yet the material. Finding suppliers for the material was not easy. Most suppliers had never heard of electrospun nanofiber, and only one supplier that we found finally did find a factory that supplied it.

The Layer Decision

The three-layer system was finalized after extensive research — polyester inner for skin contact and initial wicking, nanofiber membrane for directional transport, and nylon outer for protection and rapid evaporation.

No PFAS — By Design

Achieving water-resistance without fluorinated chemicals required choosing the right structure over the easy chemical shortcut. Every layer earns its performance through engineering, not coatings.

Hour After Hour, Day After Day

Processing thousands of papers, revising the prototype time after time, talking to supplier after supplier, the computer and ourselves both did quite a bit of work dialing in things until we got it just how we felt it would be best for wrestlers by a renowned wrestler, not just made as a general garment for anybody.

Still Going

The Research Doesn't Stop

Having a finished product on the market doesn't mean the work is done — both Mo and I continue to read current research in textiles and performance apparel — tracking new developments in fiber science, coating technologies, and moisture management. TiO₂ photocatalytic coatings, next-generation wettability gradients, improved membrane architectures — these are already on the radar.

This is not a product that was built once and left to sit. It was founded on research, and research is how it stays cutting-edge. That commitment doesn't change.
